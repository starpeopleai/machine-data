Headnote 4:3

The problem of the credibility to be accorded an accomplice's testi-
mony is one of fact (State v. Taborsky, — Conn -, 95 A2d 59, 62 (1953).
A conviction may not be based on the self-contradictory, uncertain, or
improbable testimony of an accomplice where there is no corroboration there-
of (MCM, 1951, subpar, 153a, p. 289), but if his testimony is clear, certain
and credible, it may support a conviction (Delvalley v. United States, 88
Fed 579 (7th Cir 1937). However bitterly the testimony of an accomplice
may be assailed before the court, the fact remains that it alone may support
a verdict (Caminette v. United States, 242 US 470, 496, 61 L ed 442, 27 §
Ct 192 (1917); United States v. Heitler, 274 Fed 401 (ND Ill 1921); State
ve. Guild, 10 NJL 163, 18 AM Dec 4041 (1828). Where such testimony is
corroborated by other evidence, supporting its probability and impressing
the court with a belief in its truth (and we so conclude from the record,
see infra), the conviction is even more strongly established.

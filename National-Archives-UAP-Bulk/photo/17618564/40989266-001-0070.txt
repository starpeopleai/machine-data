One hypothesis is that Mars undergoes climatic changes each
50,000-year period. At the beginning of the cycle it has an exten-
sive north polar region. It enjoys a period of spring and summer
followed by a winter period with an extensive south polar region
halfway through the cycle. Another summer period follows and
then the north polar cap grows once more. During the Martian
winters the dense atmosphere, according to this theory, is locked
in the ice caps. In the summer they melt, forming an atmosphere
that would be comfortable for us. If this is so, life on Mars may
have adapted so that it hibernates during the long winter, waiting
for the water that flows with the onset of summer to bring it back
to life. This theory will be tested when a Viking biology experi-
ment lands on Mars in 1976. The plan is to drop soil samples into
liquid water in a search for microbes.
Our investigation of Mars has already provided a wealth of
information that has changed many ideas about this fascinating
planet. Mariner 9, the space probe that circled the planet in
1971, sent back over 7000 photographs. These enabled a close
study of many features for the first time. We now know that there
are no artificial canals on the planet; but there are deep channels
which indicate that water has flowed on the surface of Mars in
the past. It now seems likely that the dark areas which astrono-
mers have seen grow and recede each year are not signs of some
form of primitive vegetation, but are windblown dust stretching
from impact craters.
Our journey through the solar system brings us next to
Jupiter, the largest of all the planets. With a diameter of 88,700
miles, it is big enough to accommodate all the other planets in
the system. It seems to be a largely gaseous body whose outer
temperatures are extremely cold-in the region of - 238°F. The
cloud belts that encircle the planet have one particular feature,
the famous Great Red Spot.
Saturn too is a giant planet that seems to be a largely gaseous
body, possibly with a hard central core. It also is a cold world-
at least as far as the outer regions are concerned, and these are
the only ones that can be studied by our present instruments.
What distinguishes it from other planets is its rings which, though
only about 10 miles in thickness, extend over 170,000 miles in
diameter.
The existence of Uranus was not discovered until after the
invention of the telescope. It is similar to Jupiter and Saturn
and its temperature is even lower at about - 346°F. Neptune is
much further out, and its existence was predicted before it was
discovered in 1846. Astronomers had correctly deduced that
deviations from the predicted orbit of Uranus were caused by
another planet, and three years later they were proved right.

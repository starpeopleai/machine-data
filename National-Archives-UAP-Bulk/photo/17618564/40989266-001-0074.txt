The stars are, in simple terms, controlled hydrogen bombs.
The source of their energy is nuclear fusion. It is thought that
stars were formed out of hydrogen and helium, which con-
tracted and compacted causing violent collisions of atoms at the
center. After a complex chain reaction, stars were formed, often
throwing off their outer skins or even exploding and causing
new stars to be born. Smaller condensations near these stars
were not large enough to produce nuclear reactions and become
stars. Instead they became planets.
It is the death of stars that is causing the most exciting con-
jecture among astronomical theorists. A star over 2½ times as
massive as our Sun is thought to collapse at the end of its life
with such force that it cannot be stopped. It contracts to one
mile across and shrinks further, crushing its matter until its
density passes that of the nucleus of an atom. As this collapse
continues the gravitational field in its vicinity increases to such
an extent that matter cannot leave the star, and light is trapped.
A photon (particle of light) traveling at the speed of light will
follow a curved path and fall back into the star. As a result the
dying star is dark. It exerts a powerful gravitational pull, but
cannot be seen. Such stars are known as "black holes." Though
at the present this is still a theoretical concept, black holes almost
certainly exist. Cygnus X-1 shows all the signs of being a binary
star-that is, a pair of stars revolving around each other-
except that only one star is visible. Yet the one that is invisible
should be 10 times the mass of the Sun and extremely bright.
Therefore it is probably a black hole.
